October 9, 2026 Coordinator | Perspective

France After the Point of No Return

The vehicle was burning. Plastic melted; a bitter smell hung in the air. The scorched metal blackened as it cooled. A firefighter lay on the asphalt. His injury proved minor—a scrape, a bruise, nothing his body would remember for long. But the vehicle burned out. In Marseille, outside the Lycée Saint-Exupéry, firefighters had come to put out a fire. They became targets themselves. The vehicle meant to save lives was reduced to ash.

Something burned with it that we take for granted until it is gone. Confidence. The quiet, unspoken confidence that when you are afraid, someone will come. Everyone needs that faith: the person fighting the fire, the person who came to protest. Convictions cannot shield a human being from flames. Fire does not ask whose side you are on. But if help itself comes under attack, what is left to count on when danger reaches you?

We need that confidence for more than fires. The children speaking about their schools need to know that someone will listen. In Grenoble, students spoke of overcrowded classrooms, teachers who were not there, an education that was losing its meaning. Some insisted their protest was peaceful. Behind that insistence, you can hear a peaceful protester's voice: “We came to raise our voices, not to throw stones.” Their demands do not become less just because someone nearby throws a stone. A just demand does not justify throwing one, either. That distinction binds us to two duties: to hear the protester and to protect the person being harmed. Neither in place of the other. Neither at the other's expense. Both. At the same time.

The distinction is hard to hold when each side sees its own loss more clearly than anyone else's. For some, the unrest makes the debate about schools inaudible; for others, the debate makes the victims invisible. Peaceful protesters are held hostage by someone else's cruelty; victims, by the rightness of someone else's position. The individual voice has nowhere to be heard. Instead of asking what a person said and what they did, people ask which group they belong to.

Freud's account of group psychology helps us understand how belonging can displace independent moral judgment. A shared emotion, identification with others, the wish to win approval from those beside you: soon, acceptance by your own group matters more than your own judgment. Refuse, and you risk being thought weak or disloyal. Agree, and you are promised an end to loneliness. The hand that acts is still yours. Little by little, though, you let the group decide how to judge what you have done.

You do not even need anonymity to surrender your judgment this way. Anonymity makes violence easier; shared conviction gives it a more secure refuge. You may be recognized, identified by name, and still believe you were right if those around you approve. The goal eclipses the harm. With so many people in agreement, doubt seems unnecessary. When you argue with yourself, your own guilt becomes easier and easier to evade: “Did I do it? Did we do it? Did anyone do it at all?”

But the decision is yours. However many people stand beside you.

Personal responsibility survives the moment when the action in the street ends and the investigation begins. Investigators must establish who did what. In Rennes, dozens of young people threw objects at police officers; near Bréquigny and Jean-Jaurès, fire vehicles were pelted with stones. Within the collective action, each person's part must be identified: what they did, in what circumstances, with what consequences. An investigation gives the act back its author. For those who attacked no one, accuracy matters as much as it does for the victims. It keeps a shared group label from making the innocent answer for someone else's guilt. It keeps collective accusation from taking the place of evidence: “You are all the same. You are all guilty. You are all the stone.”

The inquiry cannot stop with the people whose actions were visible in the street. Were there organizers behind the violence? Who financed preparations for the attacks, supplied the means, or directed the perpetrators from the shadows? Every connection must be proved. A person who took no direct part in an attack remains responsible for any role that can be established. A person who attended a protest is not guilty merely for having been there.

Suspecting an organizer, however, proves neither that such a person exists nor that they were involved. An investigation needs evidence, not a suspicion easily turned into a political accusation. If investigators pursue only those easiest to detain, the people who carried out the attacks will bear the consequences, while those who made the violence possible will retain their influence. Youth then becomes a cover for someone else's calculation. Young perpetrators will be held responsible; organizers whose involvement goes undiscovered will retain their freedom, their respectability, and the chance to do it again. Each link in that chain has its own weight, its own name. Each must be identified, each person's role established.

In La Ciotat, the teacher was fifty-nine. He was putting out a fire outside the school. Just putting out a fire. That was when someone splashed gasoline in his face. It was sticky, with a sharp smell. Then he was struck. A sixteen-year-old suspect was detained. Fifty-nine and sixteen. Neither person's age changes what happened: a man trying to stop a fire was himself attacked. The person trying to avert danger now needed protection.

In Marseille, someone used an aerosol can and a lighter to direct a flame at a woman's face. She had been trying to stop a bus shelter from being wrecked. A fifteen-year-old suspect was placed in pretrial detention. Again, two people stand before us: a minor whose future needs care, and a victim whose present has already been touched by violence. Two people. Neither makes the other disappear.

A minor's age calls for special procedures, attention to the circumstances, and a chance to change. Care for the minor does not discharge the other duty: to keep the victim from facing the consequences alone. When cruelty is explained as a youthful impulse, our sympathy slips almost unnoticed toward the young aggressor. We hear more about the teenager's future, less about the life of the person harmed. Another person's pain becomes a detail in a coming-of-age story. You cannot restore one person's future by denying another the right to protection now.

The shift begins in language. An explanation of an act becomes, almost unnoticed, an excuse for it. In Schopenhauer, the intellect can serve the will, finding arguments for what the will already desires. When we justify ourselves, reason does not lead; it finds words to serve the will. The same perspective helps us examine how violence is justified by those who commit it. The desire to feel powerful, to take revenge, to gain recognition precedes the lofty purpose later offered as an explanation. That purpose puts into words a justification for a desire already there. Not its cause. Its justification.

Ideology provides the words. Arson becomes “struggle”; an attack, “resistance”; another person's humiliation, “an answer to injustice.” The words point toward the goal and hide what was done to a human being. The act has not changed. Its author can simply live with it more easily. The inner prohibition weakens; cruelty can continue without any loss of self-respect. In the mirror, the perpetrator sees a fighter. Behind the words, the person splashed with gasoline remains unseen.

For Nietzsche, ressentiment arises from powerlessness and suppressed hostility; it produces values by negating an external adversary. What matters is not what a person builds, but whom they reject. In this conflict, we must ask whether suffering injustice comes to authorize inflicting injustice in turn. The status of victim begins to offer an excuse before a person has even acted. The debate about change recedes. To preserve a sense of moral certainty, it becomes more important to have someone to blame.

When others share that certainty, it becomes more durable than independent judgment. A group can sustain you, but its agreement does not make a decision just. The test begins where approval ends: what does your conviction permit you to do to another human being, and are you prepared to answer for it? Neither a flag nor a slogan can answer for the person acting on those convictions.

A Palestinian flag proves nothing about anyone's guilt. Neither does any other symbol or call to action. Symbols confer no moral immunity, either. If an attack is reduced to an “expression of anger,” the aggressor's words set the terms by which it is judged. The victim is pushed to the margins again. Their pain is acknowledged, but the account of what happened centers on the attacker's feelings. Language meant to bring the victim into view conceals them once more.

In Lyon, several masked people beat a municipal worker who was trying to put out a burning trash container. He was also a volunteer firefighter. He tried to avert danger, and that was what made him an obstacle. Simply keeping the flames from spreading can make you a target. You need do nothing wrong. You need only do your duty. And you are in the way.

Here Schopenhauer's compassion acquires a definite meaning: to recognize another person's suffering as no less consequential than your own, to look beyond the assertion of your own will. To see a human being where the group sees only a role—a police officer, a teacher, a municipal worker. A uniform does not erase the person wearing it. There is still a life beneath it, a life that cannot be disregarded because its wearer stands on the other side of a confrontation.

Those outraged by these attacks must also make distinctions. Accuse all schoolchildren, all university students, all migrants, all residents of the suburbs, and the individual disappears behind a collective label again. Hostility acquires a justification, this time in outrage at someone else's cruelty. A state that adopts this logic widens the conflict. Establishing who is guilty gives way to finding a collective enemy. The authorities risk reproducing the very logic of collective hostility they are obliged to stop.

To curb aggression, the authorities themselves need restraint. Firefighters and medical personnel must be able to work; citizens must be protected. The law must distinguish between a peaceful participant, a perpetrator, an instigator, and an organizer, and establish each person's role. It must check arbitrary force without allowing that effort to become arbitrary itself.

Precision is a source of state strength. It makes accountability possible by establishing guilt rather than punishing people for group membership. The legitimacy of democratic authority rests on this capacity. Citizens need to know that the law can protect them, and that they will not be made to bear someone else's guilt simply because they are called by the same group name.

The authorities are responsible for the conditions in which citizens can disagree, demand change, and stay safe. They must listen to society and protect individuals from arbitrary force at the same time. Sacrificing one duty to the other eases their work for a while, then costs them the trust of the people they exist to serve. Restoring order does not exhaust their responsibility to society.

Arrests do not make a bad school good. A police operation cannot remedy a shortage of teachers; detentions do not answer the problem of overcrowded classrooms. Stopping crime must go hand in hand with addressing the substance of the demands. Restored order allows that work to begin. It does not release the authorities from the duty to resolve the problems of education.

If those problems are forgotten, discontent will find another occasion. If attacks go unanswered, violence will become an established means of pressure. In the first case, citizens lose hope that anyone will listen; in the second, confidence that the law can protect them. Trust depends on whether the authorities have the resolve to see both tasks through when they could already declare victory. When they want to stop. When closing the matter would be easier.

The burned vehicle reminds us of what must be restored: confidence that help will come, whatever one's convictions. Holding that line means protecting the victims, establishing the responsibility of the perpetrators and everyone whose role in preparation and organization is proved, and continuing the conversation with school and university students. Democracy keeps its dignity when it sees all this work through: when it protects the freedom to seek change without placing other people's lives at the mercy of those demanding to be heard.

The confrontation in the streets must leave a society where help can reach everyone again. Anything else means surrender to the crowd, or the creation of another crowd across the barricade. To restore trust that help is possible is to restore confidence that it will come. That someone will come.

Upstairs, in an empty classroom, someone closed the window. The glass was cracked. A stone lay on the windowsill. No one had moved it. In the morning, the children will come. They will see the stone. And they will understand.

Official French Government Figures as of October 9, 2026

According to information from France's Ministry of the Interior, Ministry of Justice, and Ministry of National Education.

1. Detentions and Criminal Cases

Ministry of Justice—figures as of 5:00 p.m. on October 8, published October 9

  • Held in police custody for investigation (garde à vue): 6,877
  • Of whom minors: 5,890
  • Adults: 987
  • Subject to criminal prosecution: 2,376
  • Referred to judicial authorities: 1,547
  • Placed in pretrial detention: 21
  • Alternative measures applied: 1,897

These are official judicial statistics released by the Ministry of Justice on October 9. Detention does not amount to a finding of guilt.

Le Monde.fr

2. People Injured

Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez—remarks on October 7

  • Police officers and gendarmes: 761
  • School students: 215
  • Education personnel: 89
  • Total across these categories: 1,065

These are official cumulative figures announced by the minister on October 7. They are not a final count of people injured as of October 9.

TF1 Info

3. Property Damage

  • High schools with significant damage: 24
  • Damage in Île-de-France: €7 million
  • Damage to the Lycée Nelson-Mandela in Nantes: Approximately €2 million
  • Total damage across France: A final amount has not been published

On October 8, the government officially confirmed significant damage to 24 high schools. Regional estimates are preliminary and do not cover all damage, including damage to transportation, municipal property, and emergency service equipment.

ecologie.gouv.fr

4. Operation of Educational Institutions

According to Ministry of Education figures for the morning of October 9:

  • 141 high schools remained closed.
  • More than 96% of high schools were operating.
  • Tens of thousands of students missed classes because of damage to buildings and disruptions to school operations.

Le Monde.fr
